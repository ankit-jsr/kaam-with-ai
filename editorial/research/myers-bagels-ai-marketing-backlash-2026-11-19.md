# Myer's Bagels: when AI edits stopped looking like the real shop

**Research date:** 19 November 2026  
**Status:** Researched lead; hold for a later article or Company Page post  
**Audience:** Independent retailers, cafés, restaurants and owner-operated brands  
**Potential format:** Reported cautionary case / practical guide to AI-assisted marketing

## Editorial premise

AI can help a small business plan a content calendar or draft captions. The risk changes when it starts altering the visual evidence customers use to recognise a real shop, its products and its people.

Adam Jones, owner of Myer's Bagels in Burlington, Vermont, told Business Insider that an AI service used to help small businesses with marketing adapted the bakery's real photos into scenes that did not match the actual premises. The owner says customers noticed, some left one-star Google reviews about the posts, and he removed the posts and apologised.

The useful story is about the boundary between presentation and misrepresentation. Do not turn it into a claim that AI marketing generally damages businesses.

## What the owner describes

- The business had struggled to keep social-media posting consistent when college students who helped with the work moved on.
- Jones tried a small-business AI service that offered a calendar for a month of Instagram posts and ideas tied to local events such as graduation.
- The service could import the bakery's own photos and edit them. Jones says some AI-generated bagels and backgrounds did not look like the real products or shop.
- One post used a real retail-bag photo but placed the bags in front of a wood fire that was not the shop's fire, and turned a real customer review into a fake handwritten note.
- Another image altered a photo of the baker rolling dough by adding a wooden board, a fire and a kettle. The owner says this did not represent the shop's layout.
- The owner estimated that two posts each drew 25–30 comments, with further replies. He says some customers left one-star Google reviews about the posts rather than the food.
- He says the business removed the posts and apologised. He is not opposed to using AI elsewhere in the business, but says he will be more careful with social-media use.

## Evidence limits

We do not have:
- An independently verified count of comments or one-star reviews.
- A count of customers who stopped visiting or buying.
- Sales, revenue or profit impact from the posts.
- The name of the AI service or a technical explanation of its image-editing process.
- Direct interviews with customers who complained or customers who defended the posts.

Do not claim the incident cost the shop customers or sales. Do not imply every AI-edited image will trigger a backlash. The owner's own estimate and description should remain attributed.

## What a small business can take from it

A useful editorial checklist:

1. Use AI for planning, draft captions, variants and scheduling suggestions if those tasks help.
2. Keep product, premises, staff and process photos truthful. If an image is materially altered, label it clearly or do not use it as documentary evidence.
3. Do not turn a real customer's words into a fabricated visual testimonial. Quote the original accurately and obtain any permission needed for the intended use.
4. Check every claim in a caption: opening hours, ingredients, prices, location, awards, customer quotes and service promises.
5. If a post misleads customers, remove it and explain plainly what was wrong and what will change.

This checklist is KaamWithAI's editorial guidance derived from the incident; it is not a tested playbook or a claim about legal requirements in every jurisdiction.
