# KaamWithAI writing standard

This is an editorial standard, not an instruction to disguise AI authorship or beat AI detectors. The goal is useful, specific prose that sounds like a knowledgeable person who understands the subject and respects the reader's time.

## Core rule
Write for one real reader about one concrete problem. If a sentence could appear unchanged in 500 unrelated AI articles, it probably does not belong.

## Before drafting
1. Define the reader, their situation and the question they came to answer.
2. Find primary sources, documentation, real examples and counterarguments before outlining.
3. Decide the article's actual argument. Do not begin with a generic intro and fill a template.
4. Identify what we know, what we infer, what is hypothetical and what we have not tested.
5. Look for a useful point of view. Neutrality is not the same as insight.

## Drafting rules
- Lead with a concrete situation, surprising fact, sharp question or useful claim. No throat-clearing.
- Use plain verbs and specific nouns. Prefer "is", "does", "costs", "breaks", "saves" over "serves as", "leverages", "facilitates" and "plays a pivotal role".
- Explain mechanisms. Replace "AI can transform customer service" with the actual steps, failure modes, costs and human hand-offs.
- Vary paragraph and section length. Do not force every section into the same three-part pattern.
- Use lists only when the reader needs to scan steps, options or criteria. Do not turn every paragraph into bullets.
- Let conclusions emerge from the argument. Do not add a neat takeaway after every section or repeat the introduction at the end.
- Use first person only when it reflects an actual editorial judgement, research action or experience. Never invent first-hand testing, customers, quotes, mistakes or anecdotes.
- Use examples with enough detail to teach. Label hypothetical examples clearly.
- Include trade-offs, uncertainty, limits and cases where the recommendation does not apply.
- Write headlines that promise a specific answer, not generic transformation or urgency.
- Use diagrams, tables and original graphics when they explain a process or comparison. Do not use generic AI imagery as decoration.

## Patterns to remove on edit
- Stock openers: "In today's rapidly evolving...", "As businesses navigate...", "In an increasingly digital world..."
- Inflated importance: "pivotal", "game-changing", "revolutionary", "transformative", "critical role", "underscores the importance".
- Empty signposting: "Let's dive in", "Here's the thing", "It's important to note", "The key takeaway".
- Formulaic contrasts: "It's not about X, it's about Y" when the contrast adds no real distinction.
- Forced triads and repeated parallel lists.
- Every section ending with a summary sentence.
- Overuse of em dashes, bold lead-ins, rhetorical questions and one-line paragraphs.
- Vague sourcing: "experts say", "studies show", "research suggests" without naming the source and what it found.
- Fake precision, unsourced statistics, invented quotations and unsupported causal claims.
- Synonym-swapping that makes prose less clear.
- Repeating the same point in the intro, body, callouts and conclusion.

## Research and evidence
- Prefer official documentation, original research, government data, academic work and direct source material.
- Link sources beside the claims they support.
- Explain the population, date and limitations of statistics where relevant.
- Do not present vendor marketing claims as independent evidence.
- Do not cite a source we have not checked.
- Distinguish fact, interpretation and recommendation.

## Editing passes
1. **Argument:** What is the article really saying? Is that useful and defensible?
2. **Specificity:** Replace generalities with mechanisms, examples, numbers or remove them.
3. **Structure:** Break repetitive section patterns. Cut sections that add no new information.
4. **Voice:** Read aloud. Rewrite sentences that sound like a press release, consultant deck or generic assistant.
5. **Evidence:** Verify every factual claim, statistic, quote and link.
6. **Compression:** Remove repeated explanations, filler and summary-for-summary's-sake.
7. **Reader test:** Can a reader do, decide or understand something better after reading this?

## Final checklist
- Does the first paragraph earn the next 30 seconds?
- Does the piece have a real argument rather than a topic summary?
- Are examples concrete and honestly labelled?
- Are limitations and counterarguments included?
- Are all claims supported?
- Does the prose sound natural when read aloud?
- Could any section be deleted without losing information? If yes, delete or improve it.
- Does the page feel like KaamWithAI: clear, practical, curious, candid and visually thoughtful?

## Editorial voice
Knowledgeable operator, not breathless futurist. Curious and direct, with a point of view. Comfortable saying "we don't know", "this is overkill" or "use a spreadsheet instead". No fake founder stories, fake field tests or manufactured personality.
