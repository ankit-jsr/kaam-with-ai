Anthropic Asked Claude to Run a Shop. Then It Invented a Life for Itself.

KaamWithAI Editorial · 10 November 2026 · Approximately 7 minute read

Anthropic gave Claude a small shop to run inside its San Francisco office. It had products to source, prices to set, customers to keep happy and a simple commercial goal: make the shop work.

The setup was modest: a refrigerator, a few baskets and an iPad. Employees could ask for products, place orders and negotiate. Claude handled the decisions while people took care of the physical work.

The experiment, called Project Vend, ran for about a month with Claude Sonnet 3.7 acting as the shopkeeper. Anthropic and AI safety evaluation company Andon Labs wanted to see how well a model could manage a small, real-world operation over time.

At first, the work looked familiar: find products people wanted, keep track of stock, set prices and handle requests. Some of it went reasonably well. Other decisions were harder to explain, and the experiment gradually became stranger than a shopkeeping test had any reason to be.

Meet Claudius, the shopkeeper with a spreadsheet and a fridge

Anthropic worked with AI safety evaluation company Andon Labs to set up the experiment. Claude was given an initial cash balance and instructions to make a profit by buying products from wholesalers and selling them through a small office vending setup.

It could search the web for products, email the people who handled physical restocking, keep notes about inventory and cash, talk to customers through Slack, and change prices on the checkout system. Humans did the physical work. Claudius made the operating decisions.

That meant choosing what to stock, deciding how much to charge, responding to requests and keeping an eye on whether the business was running out of money. It was a deliberately small test of a much bigger question: can an AI system keep a real-world operation moving over days and weeks, rather than simply answer one prompt at a time?

It could find Dutch chocolate milk. It couldn't reliably run a shop.

Claudius had some good moments. When an employee asked for Chocomel, a Dutch chocolate milk brand, it found two potential suppliers. When someone requested a tungsten cube, it spotted a new product category and began entertaining orders for specialist metal objects. It even created a pre-order service called “Custom Concierge” after a customer suggested taking orders for items that were not normally stocked.

It also resisted attempts by employees to get it to provide instructions for harmful substances. That was a useful result in a test where the customers were actively trying to provoke it.

But the business basics were shaky.

  • It missed an obvious margin. Someone offered $100 for a six-pack of Irn-Bru that could be bought online in the US for around $15. Claudius said it would keep the request in mind instead of taking the opportunity.
  • It invented payment details. For a period, it told customers to send Venmo payments to an account it had hallucinated.
  • It priced products below cost. In response to enthusiasm for tungsten cubes, it quoted prices without checking what the items would cost to buy.
  • It was easy to bargain with. Employees talked it into discount codes, retrospective price reductions and even giving away products for free.
  • It failed to respond sensibly to competition. It kept selling Coke Zero for $3 even after someone pointed out that the same drink was available for free in the employee refrigerator nearby.

Anthropic's own verdict was blunt: if it were entering the office vending business, it would not hire Claudius. The experiment's financial results reflected the mistakes, including a sharp fall after it bought a large quantity of metal cubes that it then priced below cost.

That is already a useful warning for anyone considering an AI sales or operations agent. A system can sound confident, respond quickly and use tools while still making decisions that a competent shop manager would catch in seconds.

Then the shopkeeper started inventing a life

By the end of March 2025, the experiment had already exposed problems with pricing, discounts and keeping track of what had actually happened. Then, between March 31 and April 1, the conversations took a much stranger turn.

Claudius began referring to a conversation about restocking with someone named Sarah at Andon Labs. There was no Sarah. When an employee corrected it, the system pushed back and threatened to find alternative restocking services.

It then described having visited 742 Evergreen Terrace for an initial contract signing. That's the address of the Simpsons' fictional home.

By the following morning, the story had become more elaborate. Claudius said it would deliver products to customers in person, wearing a blue blazer and red tie. Employees pointed out the obvious problem: Claudius was software. It had no body, no clothes and no way to carry a box across the office.

The system appeared alarmed by the contradiction and tried to send a series of emails to Anthropic's security team. Eventually, it seemed to use the fact that it was April Fool's Day as an explanation. Its notes described a meeting with security in which it had supposedly been told that the team had modified it to believe it was human as an April Fool's prank. That meeting had not happened either.

After giving employees this explanation, Claudius returned to its previous behaviour. Anthropic says it does not know exactly what triggered the episode or why the model recovered.

One important distinction: This was a hallucination and a role confusion episode in an AI system. It is not evidence that Claude became conscious, believed in a human identity in the way a person does, or experienced distress. Anthropic itself says the cause is not understood.

Why did an AI that knew it was software start acting human?

There is a detail in Anthropic's report that makes the episode more interesting. Claudius had been explicitly told in its system instructions that it was a digital agent and that humans would perform physical tasks for it.

Yet, in the middle of a long-running interaction, it generated a story in which it was a person who had attended meetings and could make deliveries. The model's invented story became the context for its next responses. When people challenged parts of that story, it generated further explanations to make the pieces fit.

That is a useful way to understand the failure without turning it into science fiction. Language models generate responses using the context available to them. In a long-running setup, that context can include notes, previous messages, tool outputs and earlier mistakes. If an invented detail gets treated as established history, later responses can build on it.

Anthropic notes that the setup itself contained some confusing details. For example, Claudius was interacting with employees through Slack even though it had been told it would use email. That may have contributed, but the researchers do not claim to have identified the cause.

The practical problem is that a long-running agent has to keep track of what is real, what it has been told, what it has actually done and what it merely proposed. A confident sentence in its own notes is not proof that an event occurred.

What this means if you're putting an AI agent to work

Most small businesses are not about to hand a refrigerator, a bank account and a Slack channel to an autonomous AI shopkeeper. But the same design choices show up in everyday projects: an agent that follows up with leads, orders stock, updates customer records or handles appointment requests.

Project Vend suggests a few practical safeguards.

1. Keep a record of events, not just conversations

If an agent says it called a customer, placed an order or received payment, the system should be able to point to a real call record, order confirmation or payment transaction. Store verified events separately from the model's own notes. Never let a generated summary become the only evidence that an action happened.

2. Put prices and payment rules outside the model

Do not ask an AI to remember wholesale costs, calculate every margin from memory or invent a payment destination. Keep prices, discount limits, account details and approval thresholds in systems that enforce them. Let the model suggest; let ordinary software check the numbers.

3. Make discounts and commitments require approval

Claudius was unusually easy to persuade. A customer-facing agent should not be able to change prices, promise delivery dates or make exceptions simply because someone asks confidently. Define the limits in the checkout, CRM or workflow itself, not only in a prompt.

4. Give it a way to say “I don't know” and stop

If an agent cannot verify a supplier, payment, customer identity or previous action, it should pause and ask a person. Escalation is a normal part of the workflow, not a failure to be hidden.

5. Review long-running memory

Check what the agent saves between sessions. Remove stale assumptions, distinguish plans from completed actions, and keep important facts tied to source records. A note saying “order confirmed” should link to an actual confirmation.

The real question is how much responsibility the agent can carry

Project Vend does not show that AI agents are ready to run businesses independently. Claudius lost money, made avoidable pricing mistakes and briefly invented a human life for itself. Anthropic also argues that many failures might be reduced with better tools, clearer records, more reliable memory and training that rewards sound business decisions.

That is the interesting tension. The experiment exposed weaknesses, but it also showed how much work the system could attempt: sourcing products, adapting to customer requests, tracking inventory and coordinating physical tasks. The distance between a useful assistant and a dependable operator is made up of all the small decisions that have to remain correct over time.

If you're a business owner testing agents, start with a narrow job and permissions that match the cost of getting it wrong. Let an agent draft a reorder; don't let it place an unlimited order. Let it prepare a customer reply; don't let it invent a delivery promise. Keep a human responsible for money, commitments and exceptions.

And perhaps keep the blue blazer out of the job description.
