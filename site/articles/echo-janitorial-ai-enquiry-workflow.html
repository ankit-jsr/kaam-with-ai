A Cleaning Company Couldn't Answer Every Call. So It Changed the Handoff.

KaamWithAI Editorial · 19 November 2026 · Approximately 7 minute read

When a cleaning crew is inside a client's building, the owner can't always stop to answer the phone. The call may be a new customer. It may also be the next job slipping away.

Rick Chorney, co-founder of Echo Janitorial Services in Abbotsford, British Columbia, was dealing with the less glamorous side of running a service business: calls, emails, enquiries and quotes arriving while he was out doing the work.

In an interview with Fortune, Chorney described a small set of changes that began with customer intake. He connected the enquiry form to his job-management platform, added an AI receptionist and set up automatic acknowledgements for new customers. He said the initial setup took about half a day.

The interesting part isn't that he adopted a collection of AI tools. It's the sequence: capture the enquiry, get it into the system where work is managed, acknowledge it, and give routine questions somewhere to go while a person is busy.

The expensive gap between a call and a callback

A missed call is not automatically a lost customer. Someone may leave a message, call again or send a form. But every extra step gives a potential customer another chance to move on.

For a small cleaning company, this can happen at exactly the wrong moment. The owner may be inspecting a site, supervising a team, driving between jobs or speaking with a customer. The phone is ringing, but the paid work in front of them cannot simply be abandoned.

Echo's response was to make the first part of the enquiry process less dependent on the owner being available at that exact moment.

What Echo put in place

Fortune's March 2026 report describes three connected pieces:

  • Online intake: the customer enquiry form was automated so new requests flowed into Echo's job-management platform.
  • AI receptionist: Jobber's receptionist handled incoming calls, including job enquiries, and escalated matters that needed attention.
  • Automatic acknowledgement: new customers received a confirmation that their enquiry had arrived.

That last step is easy to underestimate. An acknowledgement does not win the job or answer every question. It does remove uncertainty: the customer knows the request reached the business, and the team has a record to follow up.

Chorney told Fortune the receptionist could handle up to 15 calls an hour. That's his reported experience, not a published Jobber capacity guarantee. He also described using a separate tool, Fixer AI, to sort email into action-required, drafted-reply and spam categories, then send him a daily briefing. He said he spent about 20 minutes a day on email. Those are the owner's figures; there is no independent time study in the report.

The part worth copying is the handoff

You don't need Echo's full software stack to borrow the useful idea. The core workflow for a local service business looks like this:

  1. Capture the request. Collect the customer's name, contact details, location, type of work and a short description. Ask only for information you need to decide the next step.
  2. Put it in one work queue. The enquiry should land in the system the business already uses to manage jobs, not disappear into one person's inbox. A shared spreadsheet can be enough when the volume is low.
  3. Confirm receipt. Send a short message saying the request has arrived and when the customer can expect a response. Don't promise a quote or appointment before someone has checked the details.
  4. Handle routine questions within limits. An assistant can answer approved questions about service areas, opening hours, the kinds of jobs accepted and how to request an estimate.
  5. Give exceptions to a person. Unusual jobs, complaints, urgent requests, safety issues, pricing exceptions and anything involving a firm commitment should be handed to someone who can make the decision.
  6. Track what happened next. Mark the enquiry as new, contacted, quoted, won, lost or awaiting information. That makes it possible to see where leads are actually getting stuck.

The AI receptionist is only one possible component. A simple form, shared inbox and automatic confirmation may solve most of the problem for a business receiving a handful of enquiries a day. Start there if that's enough.

What the current Jobber receptionist can do

Jobber's current Help Centre says its AI Receptionist can provide customer service over calls and texts, answer enquiries and book jobs in the calendar. The business controls what it can and cannot do. Owners can review conversation summaries, transcripts and call recordings in the Receptionist dashboard.

Jobber currently lists the feature as included with unlimited usage on its Plus plan, or as a $29 monthly add-on on eligible plans with 30 conversations included and $0.79 for each additional conversation. A dedicated Jobber phone number is required; Jobber says those numbers are currently available in the US, Canada and UK.

That availability matters. Echo is a Canadian business. An Indian cleaning or maintenance company should not assume it can sign up for the same phone workflow with a local number. Check the current country, plan and number requirements before designing a process around a particular product.

There is also a difference between what a product can do and what Echo used at the time of the Fortune interview. The article reported that Chorney paid $99 for the receptionist in March. Jobber's newer Help Centre now lists the pricing above. Those are different dated snapshots; the $99 figure should not be treated as today's standard price.

Where the human still matters

A receptionist can collect details and handle predictable questions. It cannot inspect a property, judge whether a complicated job is profitable or take responsibility for a promise the business cannot keep.

That is why the boundaries matter more than the novelty of the voice. Before turning on an AI receptionist, decide:

  • Which questions can it answer from approved business information?
  • Can it book a site visit, or should it only collect a preferred time?
  • What should happen when the customer asks for a price?
  • Which words or situations trigger an immediate human handoff?
  • Who checks the conversation log and follows up on anything unresolved?

Jobber's own documentation encourages businesses to test the receptionist before fully integrating it, review how it handles calls and adjust its settings. The dashboard also provides conversation records, which gives the owner a way to check what was said rather than relying on a summary alone.

For the first week, review every conversation. Look for wrong service information, missed urgency, confusing answers and customers who thought a quote or booking was confirmed when it wasn't. Tighten the instructions before widening the system's authority.

Did AI make Echo a million-dollar business?

Fortune reported that Echo's revenue grew from $242,000 in 2024 to just under $1 million in 2025, and that Chorney projected sales above $1.3 million for 2026. The publication says it reviewed the company's business records to verify the revenue figures.

But that does not establish that AI caused the growth. Chorney also brought sales experience, SEO work, hiring and expansion to the business. He told Fortune that AI helped him speed up quoting and hire more workers. The reporting does not isolate the contribution of each change, or show how much additional revenue came from the receptionist.

That distinction matters. A better intake process can help a business respond more consistently. It cannot, by itself, create demand, make every quote competitive or deliver good work on site.

Measure the workflow before buying more software

If you're considering something similar, take a baseline for two weeks before changing anything. Track:

  • How many calls and form enquiries arrive?
  • How many are answered or acknowledged within your target time?
  • How long does it take for a person to make the first useful contact?
  • How many enquiries turn into site visits, quotes and paid jobs?
  • How many customers need to repeat information because it was not recorded properly?

Then make one change at a time. If acknowledgement is the problem, automate acknowledgement first. If leads are scattered, fix the shared queue. If calls are regularly missed while the team is on jobs, test an answering service or AI receptionist with a narrow set of permissions.

Compare the results with the baseline. Count software costs, extra usage charges and the time someone spends checking conversations. If the system does not improve response time or lead follow-up, it has not earned a larger role.

The lesson from Echo

Echo's story is not a clean experiment proving that AI produces a particular growth rate. It is a useful example of a service business redesigning the moments between customer interest and a real person taking responsibility.

Start by making sure every enquiry gets captured, acknowledged and assigned a next step. Then decide whether AI is needed for the calls and questions that remain. The aim is not to put a bot between the customer and the business. It is to make sure the customer doesn't vanish into the gap while the owner is out doing the work.
