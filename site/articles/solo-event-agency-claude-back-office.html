The Vendor Had Replied. Claude Said They Hadn't.

KaamWithAI Editorial · 15 November 2026 · Approximately 5 minute read

The vendor had answered every question that morning. Claude said she hadn't.

The owner of a small event-staffing agency later found the answers in the full email thread. Claude had looked at the short search preview and missed the vendor's actual reply.

It sounds like a small mistake. But if you're coordinating suppliers, applicants and clients, a false report that someone has gone quiet can send the next decision in the wrong direction.

The operator described the incident in a September 2026 discussion on r/ClaudeCode. They run the agency alone and had started using Claude for email, vendor communication, the applicant database and the calendar. They said it had become a regular part of the work, though several mistakes had nearly cost the business deals.

The email preview was only the first example.

When a confident answer becomes a business problem

On another occasion, Claude said an email was “staged”. The owner went looking in Gmail for the draft. It did not exist.

Then a naming mistake became a commercial one. Claude referred to suppliers as “brand partner candidates”. The distinction mattered: a vendor gets paid by the agency; a sponsor pays the agency. The operator says they spent two days planning around an opportunity that did not exist.

Claude also said a table in the business database was missing without checking. It was there.

Each mistake involved a basic piece of business information: whether someone had replied, whether an email draft existed, what kind of relationship a company had with the agency, or whether a database record was present. The owner relied on those details to decide what to do next.

The operator saw a common pattern. Claude was stating things as facts without checking the underlying source. They said the problem became worse in long sessions, when earlier conversation started to be treated as if it were current evidence.

The rules the operator added

Rather than removing Claude from the workflow, the operator says they added a master state file to carry business context between sessions and labels to distinguish what was known from what was not:

  • CONFIRMED
  • STATED
  • ASSUMED
  • OPEN

They also added two rules: never treat an email preview as the full thread, and never claim an action happened without tool output confirming it.

The operator reports that errors fell from several per week to a few per month. That is their own estimate. They did not provide an error log or an independent audit, so it should not be read as a measured result.

The operator's changes gave Claude a clearer way to label information: what it had checked in a source, what someone had told it, what it had inferred and what was still unknown.

A persistent note can preserve a mistake, too

The comments under the discussion add an important complication. Another solo-agency operator warned that a state file can make the problem worse if the model itself writes assumptions into its permanent notes. The next session may read that assumption back as established fact.

Their suggested safeguards were stricter: only verified tool output should update durable state, entries should be dated, and factual claims should be backed by a quotation from the source. Without evidence, the system should say the claim is unverified.

Other commenters suggested shorter sessions, clearer project instructions and human approval for consequential decisions. One argued that a deterministic dashboard can be safer than relying on a language model to call and edit business systems on its own.

These are suggestions from individual practitioners, not fixes tested across different businesses. They are worth considering when an AI system carries notes from one session to the next: an old assumption can be repeated until someone checks the original record.

Check the record before acting

AI can help prepare emails, organise information and move routine work along. Before acting on its answer, check what source it used and whether the action it describes actually happened.

  1. Retrieve the source, not the preview. If the question is about an email, open the complete thread and check the latest message. If it is about a customer record, inspect the record itself.
  2. Separate proposed from completed. “I will draft the reply” is not “the draft exists”. “Ready to send” is not “sent”. Keep those states distinct.
  3. Require an action receipt. A sent email should have a message record. A calendar booking should have an event. A database change should have a confirmed result. If the tool cannot confirm it, the assistant should say so.
  4. Keep durable notes tied to evidence. Store the date, source and confidence label with important facts. Do not let an assumption quietly become permanent business context.
  5. Keep a person responsible for commitments. Money, supplier commitments, client promises, contracts and unusual exceptions should not depend on an AI-generated summary being correct.

For example, preparing a supplier email, sending it after approval and seeing it in the Sent folder are three separate steps. A business should be able to check each one.

Keep the business record as the source of truth

The operator continued using Claude, but added checks around the work it was doing.

Keep the email system, calendar and database as the records. When Claude says a supplier has not replied, open the thread. When it says a draft is ready, look for the draft in Gmail. When it reports a database change, check the record itself.

The operator says the new rules reduced the number of mistakes. We cannot independently verify that improvement, and the account does not show that the workflow is error-free. For a small business considering a similar setup, the practical steps are straightforward: keep important facts linked to their source, check that actions really happened and have a person review decisions that affect customers or money.
